⚡ What This Calculator Does

This tool estimates expected oil loss using a semi-empirical model calibrated from real-world user data. Inputs include engine type, riding style, RPM band, oil viscosity, operating temperature, odometer reading, and engine state. The model outputs estimated consumption rate (ml/1000 km), total loss over a distance, and a status assessment against known normal ranges. It is a directional tool — not a substitute for physically checking your oil level.

Formula: Oil Loss = k × RPM^a × Load^b × (1/Viscosity^c) × exp((T−T_ref)/60) × WearFactor × OdoFactor
Constants calibrated per engine using aggregated user-reported consumption data. See "How the Model Works" tab for full breakdown.

Affects wear factor — higher mileage = more consumption
Disclaimer: This calculator is a directional estimate based on the semi-empirical model. It does NOT account for manufacturing defects, seal failures, sudden mechanical faults, or abnormal blow-by from ring damage. Real-world variation is high. Always physically check your oil level every 500–1,000 km regardless of this estimate. Values for Triumph TR-series are based on limited owner data (2023–2025) and carry higher uncertainty than the more mature 373cc dataset.

Factor Breakdown
HOW EACH INPUT MULTIPLIER IS CALCULATED

RPM Factor

RPM directly controls piston velocity. Higher piston speed increases the gap between rings and cylinder wall during the power stroke, allowing more blow-by of combustion gases and oil vapor.

Formula: RPM_Factor = (RPM_input / 7000)^1.6
At 7000 RPM → factor = 1.0 (reference). At 9000 RPM → factor ≈ 1.54. At 5000 RPM → factor ≈ 0.64.

Load Factor (Riding Style)

Load = ratio of actual engine output to maximum possible output. City riding at moderate throttle = ~25–40% load. Highway = ~30–50%. Aggressive/track = 70–100%.

Load map: City = 0.30 · Highway = 0.42 · Mixed = 0.36 (reference) · Aggressive = 0.78
Formula: Load_Factor = (load / 0.36)^0.85

Viscosity Factor

Thinner oils (lower viscosity) have weaker film strength. At high temperatures, 10W-30 thins further than 15W-50, allowing more oil to pass piston rings. Multiple KTM 390 users correlated Motul 7100 (a fully-synthetic, lower-viscosity product) with higher consumption.

Viscosity map (cSt @ 80°C): 10W-30 = 10.5 · 10W-40 = 14.2 · 10W-50 = 17.8 (reference) · 15W-50 = 19.5
Formula: Visc_Factor = (17.8 / visc)^0.55

Temperature Factor

Higher oil temperatures reduce film strength and increase oil vapor pressure, both contributing to higher combustion-chamber oil entry.

Reference: 95°C → factor = 1.0
Formula: Temp_Factor = exp((T_input − 95) / 60)
At 120°C → factor ≈ 1.51. At 75°C → factor ≈ 0.72.

Wear Factor (Engine State)

New engines have tighter tolerances but rings are still seating; aged engines have worn rings and looser tolerances. Both extremes increase consumption vs a well-run-in engine.

Map: New/break-in = 1.30 · Run-in = 1.00 (reference) · Mid-life = 1.22 · Aged = 1.60

Odometer Factor

A continuous wear nudge that increases consumption with cumulative mileage, independent of engine state classification. Captures progressive wear at bore, rings, and valve stems.

Formula: Odo_Factor = 1.0 + min(0.30, odometer_km / 300,000)
At 0 km → 1.0. At 30,000 km → 1.10. At 90,000 km → 1.30 (maximum +30%).

Model Assumptions & Known Limitations
FROM THE OIL CONSUMPTION MODEL METHODOLOGY — VERIFIED APRIL 2026

⚡ About This Model

The oil consumption model is semi-empirical: physically motivated but with constants calibrated from real-world user data. It is suitable for directional comparison (KTM vs Dominar; city vs highway; oil grade effects) — not for exact prediction. Engine condition, ring wear, and manufacturing variance mean actual consumption can deviate significantly from model output.

Why does the model use RPM as a primary variable?+
RPM directly controls piston velocity. Higher piston speed increases the gap between rings and cylinder wall during the power stroke, allowing more blow-by of combustion gases and oil vapor. On the 373cc platform, aggressive high-RPM use (8,000–10,000 RPM on KTM 390) exacerbates this far beyond what a commuter single-cylinder sees. The model uses RPM^a where 'a' (= 1.6) is empirically fitted from user-reported data across riding styles.
What is "Load" in this context, and how is it estimated?+
Load = ratio of actual engine output to maximum possible output. City riding at moderate throttle = ~25–40% load. Highway cruising at steady speed = ~30–50%. Aggressive / track riding = 70–100%. The model approximates this from riding style classification. Real load measurement requires a dyno or instrumented ECU data. The model assumes load reflects average sustained load, not peak momentary values.
Why does viscosity affect oil consumption?+
Thinner oils (lower viscosity) have weaker film strength. At high temperatures, a 10W-30 oil thins further than a 15W-50, allowing more oil to pass piston rings and enter the combustion chamber. Additionally, thinner fully-synthetic oils (like Motul 7100) have been reported by multiple KTM 390 users as correlating with higher consumption — either due to film thinning or thermal evaporation of lighter base stock fractions. The model applies a (1/Viscosity^c) factor to reflect this.
What does the engine-specific constant 'k' represent?+
'k' captures all engine-specific factors not explicitly modeled: compression ratio, ring seal quality, combustion chamber design, valve stem seal condition, and tuning aggressiveness. KTM 390 is assigned a higher k (75) than Dominar 400 (35) because its aggressive tune, higher compression, and redline targets create more blow-by opportunity. The Dominar 400 has the same block but a lower k due to its detuned state. In real calibration, k would be fit using regression against collected user data (oil consumed over known distance at known conditions).
What does the model NOT capture?+
Manufacturing tolerances: A bike with looser ring fit from the factory will consume more oil regardless of riding style. Sudden failures: Water pump seal failure, gasket failure, or piston ring damage create step-change increases the steady-state model cannot predict. External leaks: Crankcase gasket seeps, oil cooler leaks, and rocker cover leaks are not included. Altitude and humidity: These affect combustion efficiency and oil film behavior. Engine age and cumulative wear: Partially captured by Odo_Factor, but not with precision.
Why is the 300–500 ml / 1,000 km figure reported by some users so much higher than the model suggests?+
Cases of 300–500 ml per 1,000 km almost certainly indicate a mechanical fault beyond normal ring blow-by. Likely culprits: failing valve stem seals allowing oil to drain into the combustion chamber when the engine is off (explains higher consumption on restarts); crankcase breather hose overflow depositing oil into the airbox; early-stage ring damage from coolant contamination; or an incipient water pump seal failure introducing coolant that displaces the oil level reading. These are failures, not normal consumption, and should be investigated as warranty/service issues.
Is KTM's "100–200 ml / 1,000 km is acceptable" claim defensible?+
This is disputed. For a high-performance 373cc single operating at 8,000–9,000 RPM frequently, 100 ml per 1,000 km may be defensible at the upper limit of "acceptable" per most manufacturer standards (roughly 1 qt per 1,500 miles is the Bell Performance industry reference). However, 200 ml per 1,000 km starts to approach the outer limit of what industry standards consider acceptable even for performance engines. The fact that KTM had to issue a circular specifically about this suggests the rate exceeded what owners expected — pointing to either a manufacturing variance issue or a tuning decision that should have come with better owner communication. Veteran KTM mechanics from outside India report far lower consumption (under 100 ml per 7,500 km) on well-maintained Gen 1 bikes, supporting the argument that post-2019 bikes have a genuine piston ring / engine design issue.
How confident are the Triumph TR-series values?+
The Triumph TR-series (2023 onwards) has a much shorter on-road history than the KTM 373cc platform, and fewer high-mileage units exist as reference. The base rate of 28 ml/1000km and the normalMax of 50 ml/1000km are extrapolated from Triumph 400 Forum and Team-BHP owner reports (2023–2025). These values carry higher uncertainty than the 373cc dataset, which benefits from years of accumulated forum data across thousands of owners. As more TR-series bikes reach 30,000–50,000 km, these calibration figures should be revisited.

MODEL CALIBRATION METHODOLOGY NOTE

In the absence of controlled laboratory measurements, the model constants (k, exponents a, b, c) are calibrated by fitting the model's output to clusters of user-reported consumption rates. The approach is: identify a "normal" cluster of reported values at known riding conditions → treat this as the reference point → calibrate constants to reproduce this cluster. This is a recognized approach for semi-empirical engineering models when first-principles data is unavailable. The model should be recalibrated when larger controlled datasets become available.
