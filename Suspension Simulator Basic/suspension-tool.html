01

Single degree of freedom

The system is modelled as one mass moving along one axis only. In reality, a motorcycle suspension has 6+ DOF — pitch, roll, yaw, and coupled front/rear kinematics all interact.

02

Linear viscous damping

Damping force = c · ẋ. Force is proportional to velocity. Real dampers are nonlinear — oil flow through small orifices follows the square-root of pressure drop, and shim stacks have progressive opening characteristics.

03

Linear spring (Hookean)

Spring force = k · x. Constant rate across full stroke. Real fork springs and shock springs are often progressive (rate increases with compression) or use helper springs for initial plushness.

04

Zero external forcing

RHS = 0. We model only the free oscillation after an impulse — like a single bump. Continuous road inputs require a forced response model with road profile as F(t), typically using PSD analysis.

05

Constant coefficients

m, c, and k do not vary with time, position, velocity, or temperature. In practice, oil viscosity drops with heat (fade), spring rate changes with preload, and mass shifts under braking and acceleration.

06

No unsprung mass

Tyre, wheel, and brake assembly mass (unsprung) are ignored. Real quarter-car models use two masses — sprung (chassis) and unsprung (wheel) — connected by the spring/damper, with the tyre as a second spring.

07

No tyre dynamics

Tyre is assumed rigid contact with ground. Real tyres have stiffness (~150–200 N/mm sidewall) and damping — they act as a second suspension stage, heavily affecting grip over small-frequency vibrations.

08

Symmetric damping

c is identical in compression and rebound. Real shocks have separate compression (bump) and rebound adjuster circuits — intentionally asymmetric. Rebound is usually 2–3x slower than compression to prevent pack-down.

Why this model still matters

Despite these simplifications, the SDOF viscous damping model correctly predicts regime behaviour — underdamped, critically damped, overdamped — and the relationship between ζ and oscillation decay rate. All real suspension tuning is rooted in understanding these regimes first. The model gives you the mental framework; real shocks then add nonlinear corrections on top.

Real motorcycle suspension is typically tuned to ζ ≈ 0.25–0.40 (underdamped). Critical damping sounds ideal in theory but feels wooden and kills mechanical grip in practice — the wheel can't track small road irregularities fast enough.

Derived parameters

ωₙ Natural frequency = √(k/m)   [rad/s] — how fast it oscillates with no damping
ζ Damping ratio = c / (2√(mk)) — dimensionless, defines the regime
ωd Damped natural freq = ωₙ√(1−ζ²) — actual oscillation frequency when underdamped
cc Critical damping coefficient = 2√(mk) — minimum c for no oscillation

Solution method — characteristic equation

01

Assume solution form

x(t) = e^(λt)

Substituting into the ODE gives a characteristic polynomial in λ

02

Characteristic polynomial

mλ² + cλ + k = 0

Quadratic in λ — two roots determine the response type

03

Roots via quadratic formula

λ = (−c ± √(c² − 4mk)) / 2m
λ = −ζωₙ ± ωₙ√(ζ² − 1)

Discriminant c² − 4mk determines whether roots are real or complex

04

Apply initial conditions

x(0) = x₀ = 1    ẋ(0) = v₀ = 0

Constants A and B in the general solution are fixed by these two conditions

Underdamped

ζ < 1  ·  c² < 4mk

Complex conjugate roots
λ = −ζωₙ ± iωd

x(t) = e^(−ζωₙt)
 ·[A·cos(ωd·t)
 + B·sin(ωd·t)]

Oscillates and decays.
Bike bounces over bump.

01

Mechanical Vibrations — Rao, S.S.

Pearson, 6th Edition (2017)  ·  Chapter 2: Free Vibration of Single-DOF Systems

Primary reference for the SDOF equation of motion, characteristic equation derivation, and all three damping regime solutions. Standard undergraduate textbook used in mechanical engineering programs globally.

02

Engineering Vibration — Inman, D.J.

Pearson, 4th Edition (2013)  ·  Chapter 1

Alternative derivation of damping ratio and natural frequency. Cleaner treatment of initial conditions applied to the three solution cases. Used to verify the solution forms shown in the Formula tab.

03

Motorcycle Dynamics — Vittore Cossalter

2nd Edition (2006)  ·  Chapter 4: Suspensions

Source for real-world motorcycle suspension parameters — sprung mass ranges, spring rate values, and typical damping ratios used in production motorcycles. Explains why ζ ≈ 0.25–0.40 is preferred over critical damping for grip.

04

Race Tech's Motorcycle Suspension Bible — Thede & Parks

Motorbooks, 2010

Practical source for compression/rebound asymmetry, shim stack behaviour, and why real dampers deviate from linear viscous damping. Explains the square-root relationship between oil velocity and damping force through orifices.

05

Vehicle Dynamics — Fundamentals and Limit Handling — Milliken & Milliken

SAE International, 1995  ·  Chapter 16

Quarter-car model derivation including unsprung mass. Explains why ignoring unsprung mass is a significant simplification and how wheel hop frequency (~10–15 Hz) interacts with body resonance (~1–2 Hz) in real vehicles.

06

Tyre and Vehicle Dynamics — Pacejka, H.B.

Butterworth-Heinemann, 3rd Edition (2012)

Source for tyre stiffness values (~150–200 N/mm radial) and tyre damping characteristics used to justify the no-tyre-dynamics assumption in the Assumptions tab.
